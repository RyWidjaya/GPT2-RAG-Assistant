Lee Sang-hyeok, known worldwide as Faker, was born on May 7, 1996, in Seoul, South Korea. He rose through the Korean solo queue ladder as a teenager, and his performances there caught the attention of professional scouts. In 2013 he signed with SK Telecom T1 and made his professional debut as the team's mid laner. Within months he had become the centerpiece of the roster and one of the most talked-about newcomers in the history of the game.

Faker's first major triumph came at the 2013 World Championship, where SK Telecom T1 defeated Royal Club in the final held in Los Angeles. He was only seventeen years old. He won his second World Championship in 2015 against KOO Tigers, and his third in 2016 against Samsung Galaxy. Those three titles established the team as the first dynasty of professional League of Legends. Fans began calling him the Unkillable Demon King because of his ability to survive and win fights that other players would have lost.

After 2016 the team entered a long stretch without a world title. Many observers assumed his peak had passed, since esports careers are usually short and reaction times decline with age. Faker responded by reinventing his role. He shifted from a pure carry into a strategic leader who set the tempo of the whole team, and he kept adapting to new champions and new patches every season.

In 2023 he lifted his fourth World Championship trophy, with T1 beating Weibo Gaming in Seoul, seven years after his previous one. In 2024 the team won again, defeating Bilibili Gaming in London in a close five-game series, and in 2025 T1 took a third consecutive title against KT Rolster. That gives Faker a record six World Championship titles, more than any other player in the history of the game.

Outside of Worlds, Faker has won the Mid-Season Invitational twice, in 2016 and 2017, along with a large number of LCK titles in the Korean domestic league. He is also known for a wide champion pool. Over the years he has been associated with LeBlanc, Ryze, Azir, Syndra, Orianna and many others, and he was among the first mid laners to show how flexible a single role could be.

His playstyle is defined by mechanical precision, calm decision making and strong map awareness. Teammates and coaches often say his greatest strength is not his hands but his reading of the game: he seems to know where the enemy jungler is before the jungler appears. Opponents have described facing him as playing against someone who is always one step ahead.

Faker is also famous for loyalty. He has spent his entire career with one organization, T1, formerly SK Telecom T1, and has repeatedly turned down offers from other teams and other regions. Fans regard this as remarkable in an industry where players often change teams every year. His longevity has become part of his legend, and he is widely regarded as the best League of Legends player of all time.

In 2024 he became the first inductee of the League of Legends Hall of Legends, an honor created to recognize players whose careers shaped the sport. His influence goes beyond trophies. He has brought new audiences to esports, inspired a generation of players in Korea and abroad, and is often compared with figures such as Michael Jordan in basketball or Lionel Messi in football for the way he defines his sport.

T1 is a South Korean esports organization with teams in several games, best known for its League of Legends roster. The organization began in 2004 as a StarCraft team backed by the telecommunications company SK Telecom, and it later expanded into other titles as competitive gaming grew in Korea. It entered League of Legends in 2012 under the name SK Telecom T1.

The team became a powerhouse quickly. After the 2013 World Championship win, it developed a reputation for disciplined practice, strong coaching and an emphasis on teamwork. Coach Kim Jeong-gyun, known as kkOma, played a major role in building the culture of the team and in guiding young players through the pressures of professional play.

In 2019 the organization rebranded as T1 after a restructuring of its ownership. A joint venture involving SK Telecom and Comcast Spectacor helped fund the new entity. The name changed, but the players, the fan base and the winning tradition continued. Supporters still refer to the team by nicknames from the SK Telecom era.

The T1 League of Legends roster typically fields five starters: a top laner, a jungler, a mid laner, a bot laner and a support. Over the years the roster has included many stars, but the mid lane has always been anchored by Faker. Younger players have joined and left, and the team has repeatedly rebuilt around him while keeping a consistent identity.

T1 competes in the LCK, the League of Legends Champions Korea, which is the top league in the country. The team plays split seasons, followed by playoffs, and the best Korean teams qualify for the World Championship each autumn. Regional rivals such as Gen.G, DRX and KT Rolster provide some of the most-watched matches on the calendar.

Off the stage, T1 runs a large content and merchandise operation. It produces documentaries, streams player practice sessions, and works with sponsors across technology, food and fashion. The organization also operates an academy system that develops young talent, so that future stars can be trained in the team's style before reaching the main roster.

The fan culture around T1 is among the most passionate in esports. Arenas in Seoul often sell out within minutes, and international fans travel to watch matches in person. Chants, banners and fan art are a major part of the atmosphere. Many fans grew up watching the 2013 and 2015 teams and now bring their own children to games.

League of Legends is a multiplayer online battle arena game developed by Riot Games and released in 2009. Two teams of five players face each other on a map called Summoner's Rift. Each player controls a single character, called a champion, and the goal is to destroy the enemy team's Nexus, a large structure located at the heart of their base.

The map has three lanes: top, middle and bottom. Between the lanes lies the jungle, an area filled with neutral monsters that can be killed for gold and experience. Each lane is guarded by defensive towers, and a team must destroy towers to push forward. Small computer-controlled units called minions march down each lane, and killing them provides income.

Every team has five roles. The top laner is usually a durable fighter or tank. The jungler moves between lanes to help teammates and secure objectives. The mid laner often plays a mage or assassin with high damage. The bot laner, also called the attack damage carry, deals sustained damage from range. The support protects and enables the carry with healing, shields or crowd control.

Objectives on the map give strong advantages. The dragons provide permanent bonuses to the team that kills them, and the fourth or fifth dragon can grant a powerful buff. Baron Nashor, a large monster in the top half of the river, grants a temporary power boost that helps a team break through defenses. The Rift Herald and void grubs help teams take towers early in the game.

Champions gain levels by earning experience and buy items with gold to become stronger. There are more than one hundred and fifty champions, each with a unique set of abilities. Players choose champions in a draft phase, where teams take turns banning and picking, and the draft itself is a strategic contest that can decide a match before it begins.

A typical professional game lasts between twenty-five and forty minutes. Early on, teams focus on laning, farming and small skirmishes. In the middle of the game, they group for team fights and objective control. Late in the game, a single mistake can lose everything, because the strongest champions can end the game in one decisive fight.

Riot Games organizes professional play through regional leagues and international events. The Mid-Season Invitational brings together regional champions in the spring, and the World Championship, the most prestigious event, takes place each autumn and crowns the world champion. Prize pools and viewership have grown steadily, with finals drawing millions of live viewers.

Esports is organized competitive video gaming, and it has grown from small tournaments in the late 1990s into a global industry. Major titles include League of Legends, Dota 2, Counter-Strike, Valorant and StarCraft. Professional teams sign players to contracts, employ coaches and analysts, and compete in leagues and tournaments that are streamed to large online audiences.

South Korea played an early and important role. Korean broadcasters televised StarCraft championships in the early 2000s, and star players became celebrities. Internet cafes called PC bangs made competitive gaming a mainstream social activity. That environment produced generations of professional players and a training culture that other regions later copied.

The four major regions in League of Legends are Korea, China, Europe and North America. Korean teams have won the most World Championships, though Chinese teams have won several championships as well, and European teams have won it at least once. Strong regional rivalries drive interest, and the international events are often described as the Olympics of esports.

A professional esports player's career is usually short. Reaction speed peaks in the early twenties, and many players retire before thirty. The workload is heavy, with ten to fourteen hours of practice per day, including scrims against other teams, individual solo queue, and review sessions. Teams now employ sports psychologists, physical trainers and nutritionists to protect the health of their players.

Compared with traditional sports, esports has some unusual features. The games are owned by private companies that can change the rules every two weeks through a patch. A champion that dominates one month may be weakened the next. Players who succeed over a long period must relearn the game constantly, which is one reason longevity is considered such an achievement.

Championships in traditional sports are decided by long seasons and playoffs, and esports has borrowed the same structure. In football, the championships of the major leagues attract billions of viewers. In basketball, the championships of the NBA turn players such as Michael Jordan into global icons. Esports championships are smaller, but the fan devotion is comparable, and the biggest finals now fill stadiums.

Sponsorship and media rights provide much of the money in esports. Companies in technology, energy drinks, banking and automotive sectors sponsor teams and events. Prize pools can reach millions of dollars, though salaries and streaming revenue are a larger source of income for many top players.

Inside the chloroplasts of plant cells, a pigment called chlorophyll absorbs sunlight, mostly in the red and blue parts of the spectrum.
